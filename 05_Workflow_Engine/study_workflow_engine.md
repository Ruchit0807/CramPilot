# Study Workflow Engine
### Exam-Clutch — Complete Design Document

---

## Overview

The Study Workflow Engine (SWE) is the operational core of Exam-Clutch. Where the Professor Intelligence System understands the examiner and the AI Routing System selects the tools, the Workflow Engine orchestrates everything into an executable, hour-by-hour plan that a student can follow without making another strategic decision.

The engine's fundamental insight is this: the reason students fail despite using AI tools is not that the tools are bad — it is that students have no system for sequencing their use. They open ChatGPT, generate a summary, read it, feel vaguely prepared, open a different tool, repeat. The Workflow Engine replaces this chaos with a sequence engineered for the specific combination of subject, professor, time constraint, and target outcome.

Every workflow generated by this engine has five properties:
- **Bounded** — the student always knows how long the current task takes
- **Ordered** — tasks follow a research-backed sequence (understand → memorize → practice → evaluate → consolidate)
- **Calibrated** — depth and breadth are set by target marks, not by syllabus coverage
- **Adaptive** — the workflow adjusts when the student falls behind, discovers new gaps, or completes a phase early
- **Professor-aware** — the output format matches what this specific professor rewards

# Part 1: The Seven Core Workflows

---

## Workflow 1 — 24-Hour Exam Survival

**Trigger conditions:** Exam in 18–28 hours. Student has not started or has partial preparation. Applicable to any subject, any professor type.

**Design philosophy:** At T–24 hours, the most dangerous thing a student can do is try to cover everything. The second most dangerous is trying to understand everything they cover. The 24-hour workflow is built on brutal triage, high-speed surface coverage, and converting understood content into retrievable memory through active testing — not re-reading.

---

### Master Timeline

```
TOTAL: 20 active study hours (leaving 4 hours for sleep/rest)

PHASE 1   TRIAGE                    0:00 – 0:45    (45 min)
PHASE 2   CRITICAL TOPIC COVERAGE   0:45 – 8:45    (8 hours)
PHASE 3   ACTIVE RECALL TESTING     8:45 – 12:45   (4 hours)
PHASE 4   GAP REPAIR                12:45 – 15:45  (3 hours)
PHASE 5   MEMORIZATION SPRINT       15:45 – 18:45  (3 hours)
PHASE 6   EXAM SIMULATION           18:45 – 20:45  (2 hours)
PHASE 7   CONSOLIDATION             20:45 – 21:45  (1 hour)
SLEEP/REST                          21:45 – EXAM
```

---

### Phase 1: Triage (45 minutes)

**Purpose:** Determine exactly what matters. Do not start content until this is complete.

**Tool:** ChatGPT

**Task sequence:**
1. Input the full syllabus or topic list into ChatGPT
2. Input any available past paper information
3. Run the triage prompt

**Prompt:**
```
"I have 24 hours before my [SUBJECT] exam at [UNIVERSITY LEVEL].
 My syllabus covers: [PASTE SYLLABUS].
 Past paper patterns suggest: [PASTE ANY KNOWN PATTERNS].
 My professor's style is: [DESCRIBE IF KNOWN].

 Classify every topic into:
 CRITICAL — High exam probability, significant marks weight
 MODERATE — Likely to appear, worth partial preparation
 LOW — Unlikely, or low marks, prepare only if time allows
 SKIP — Very unlikely; do not invest time

 Then give me:
 1. The hour-by-hour sequence I should follow
 2. The maximum time to spend per critical topic
 3. The 3 topics where I can score the most marks fastest"
```

**Output to generate:**
- Tiered topic list (Critical / Moderate / Low / Skip)
- Hour-by-hour study schedule
- Quick wins list (topics where effort-to-marks ratio is highest)

**Completion signal:** You have a prioritized list and a schedule. Do not proceed to Phase 2 without completing this.

---

### Phase 2: Critical Topic Coverage (8 hours)

**Purpose:** Build sufficient understanding of Critical topics to write an exam answer. Not deep mastery — exam-functional understanding.

**Tools:** ChatGPT (primary), Claude (for genuinely complex concepts)

**Time allocation per topic:**
- Critical topics: 45–60 minutes each
- Moderate topics: 20–30 minutes each
- Low topics: 10 minutes (skim only)

**Per-topic sequence (45-minute topic block):**

```
MINUTES 0–10: Concept load
Tool: ChatGPT
Prompt: "In 200 words, explain [TOPIC] for a university exam.
         Cover: the core concept, one worked example or case,
         the most likely exam question format, and 3 key terms
         I must know. Be concise — I have 10 minutes."

MINUTES 10–25: Depth check (only if concept is unclear)
Tool: Claude (switch only if ChatGPT output left genuine confusion)
Prompt: "Explain why [CONCEPT] works this way.
         I understand [WHAT YOU GOT], but I don't understand
         [WHAT CONFUSED YOU]. Explain the underlying logic in
         under 150 words."

MINUTES 25–40: Question exposure
Tool: ChatGPT
Prompt: "Give me 3 exam-style questions on [TOPIC] at
         [MARKS] marks each. Include model answer outlines —
         not full answers, just the key points each answer
         must hit."

MINUTES 40–45: Self-test
Close the tool. Write from memory: what is this topic about,
what's the key concept, what's the likely question.
If you can't do this, spend 5 more minutes and repeat.
```

**Phase 2 warning rules:**
- Never spend more than 90 minutes on any single topic regardless of its importance
- If you're still confused after 60 minutes: mark it as "partial" and move on
- Do not take notes in the traditional sense — write only key terms and question frameworks. Full note-writing costs too much time.

---

### Phase 3: Active Recall Testing (4 hours)

**Purpose:** Convert understood content into retrievable memory. This phase is non-negotiable. Students who skip it for more coverage perform significantly worse than students who do less coverage and test more.

**Tool:** Claude (primary), ChatGPT (for volume testing)

**Sequence:**

```
BLOCK A (90 min): Topic-by-topic rapid testing
Tool: ChatGPT
Prompt: "Quiz me on [ALL TOPICS COVERED IN PHASE 2].
         Ask one question at a time. Wait for my answer.
         After my answer: score it, tell me what I missed,
         then move to the next question.
         Start with the topic I'm least confident about."

BLOCK B (90 min): Answer quality evaluation
Tool: Claude
For each Critical topic:
Write a practice answer (8–10 minutes) without notes.
Then:
Prompt to Claude: "I'm preparing for [SUBJECT] exam.
                   Here is a [MARKS]-mark exam question: [QUESTION]
                   Here is my answer: [YOUR ANSWER]
                   Mark it strictly. Show marks per component.
                   Identify: what's missing, what's imprecise,
                   what would score highest for this type of professor:
                   [PROFESSOR TYPE]."

BLOCK C (60 min): Gap list construction
Based on testing results, build a specific list:
"I failed or scored poorly on: [LIST SPECIFIC GAPS]"
This list drives Phase 4.
```

---

### Phase 4: Gap Repair (3 hours)

**Purpose:** Targeted learning for specific gaps identified in Phase 3 only. Not general coverage. Not new topics. Specific identified weaknesses only.

**Tool:** Claude (primary — best for targeted gap resolution)

**Sequence:**

```
For each gap on your list from Phase 3:

STEP 1: Targeted explanation (15–20 min per gap)
Prompt: "I failed to answer questions about [SPECIFIC GAP].
         Here is what I think I understood: [YOUR UNDERSTANDING].
         Here is what I got wrong: [YOUR ERRORS].
         Explain exactly what I'm misunderstanding.
         Then give me the simplest way to remember the correct version."

STEP 2: Immediate re-test (5 min per gap)
Prompt: "Now ask me the question again differently.
         Don't accept a vague answer."

STEP 3: Move on
If you still can't answer it after two attempts:
accept this gap, note it, and allocate 0 more time to it.
Some gaps cannot be closed in 24 hours. Acknowledge them.
Protect time for closable gaps.
```

---

### Phase 5: Memorization Sprint (3 hours)

**Purpose:** Convert all studied content into fast-access recall memory using structured memorization systems calibrated to the output format the professor requires.

**Tools:** ChatGPT (mnemonic/flashcard generation), NotebookLM (if notes uploaded)

**Memorization method by professor type:**

```
DEFINITION-FOCUSED PROFESSOR:
  Tool: NotebookLM (from uploaded notes) or ChatGPT
  Method: Keyword extraction flashcards
  Prompt: "From the following content: [PASTE NOTES],
           extract the 20 most important definitions I must
           know for tomorrow. For each: the term, the precise
           definition, and the 3 marker words the examiner
           will look for."

THEORY-HEAVY PROFESSOR:
  Tool: ChatGPT
  Method: Framework narrative
  Prompt: "Convert the core argument of [THEORY] into a
           memorable story or sequence I can tell myself
           in 2 minutes before writing an essay answer."

NUMERICAL PROFESSOR:
  Tool: ChatGPT
  Method: Formula and problem-type triggers
  Prompt: "Give me a formula sheet for [TOPIC] with:
           each formula, when to use it (in plain English),
           and the most common error students make with it."

PYQ-REPEATING PROFESSOR:
  Tool: Claude / NotebookLM
  Method: Model answer memorization
  Focus: Memorize the structure and key points of the top
         5 predicted questions — not word for word, but the
         exact components that earn marks.

APPLICATION PROFESSOR:
  Tool: ChatGPT
  Method: Framework-trigger memorization
  Prompt: "For [TOPIC], give me: the framework to apply,
           the 3 keywords that tell me to use this framework,
           and a one-paragraph template I can adapt for any
           scenario question."
```

---

### Phase 6: Exam Simulation (2 hours)

**Purpose:** Simulate exam conditions to prime recall and identify final gaps. Build confidence through successful performance.

**Tools:** Claude (for evaluation), ChatGPT (for question generation)

**Sequence:**

```
0:00–0:15: Question selection
Generate (ChatGPT) or select from past papers:
- 2 Critical topic questions
- 1 Moderate topic question
- 1 wildcard (topic you feel least certain about)

0:15–1:15: Timed writing
Write all 4 answers. No tools open. No notes. Time yourself.
Simulate the exam hall.

1:15–1:45: Evaluation
Paste all answers to Claude:
Prompt: "I am simulating my exam for tomorrow.
         Here are my 4 answers: [PASTE].
         Here are the questions: [PASTE].
         Mark each one. Tell me: which ones would pass,
         which would fail, what's missing from each,
         and what's the single most important improvement
         I can make in the remaining time."

1:45–2:00: Act on feedback
Make one targeted fix per answer. One. Not a complete rewrite.
```

---

### Phase 7: Consolidation (1 hour)

**Purpose:** Cement what's been learned. Do not attempt new material.

**Tools:** ChatGPT (for rapid review only)

```
FINAL REVIEW SEQUENCE:

15 min: Key terms sweep
Mentally run through every key term from every Critical topic.
For any term that feels uncertain: write it on paper once.

15 min: Question structure review
For each Critical topic: recall the likely question format
and the 3 main points your answer must hit.

15 min: Marks trap review
If professor profile has known marks traps
(missing conclusions, imprecise terminology, unlabeled diagrams),
review these explicitly. Write them on paper.

15 min: Rest preparation
Stop all tool use. Put phone/laptop away.
Prepare physically for tomorrow. Eat. Hydrate.
```

## Workflow 2 — 48-Hour Exam Survival

**Trigger conditions:** Exam in 36–56 hours. Partial or no preparation.

**Design philosophy:** 48 hours enables genuine understanding, not just surface coverage. The additional time allows a proper understand-then-practice loop for each topic rather than the skim-then-test compromise of the 24-hour workflow.

---

### Master Timeline

```
DAY 1 (24 hours before exam)

PHASE 1   DEEP TRIAGE               0:00 – 1:00    (1 hour)
PHASE 2   CRITICAL TOPIC MASTERY    1:00 – 9:00    (8 hours)
PHASE 3   MODERATE TOPIC COVERAGE   9:00 – 13:00   (4 hours)
PHASE 4   ACTIVE RECALL             13:00 – 17:00  (4 hours)
PHASE 5   PROFESSOR CALIBRATION     17:00 – 19:00  (2 hours)
REST / SLEEP                        19:00 – 07:00

DAY 2 (exam day or day before)

PHASE 6   GAP REPAIR                7:00 – 10:00   (3 hours)
PHASE 7   ANSWER QUALITY TRAINING   10:00 – 14:00  (4 hours)
PHASE 8   EXAM SIMULATION           14:00 – 16:00  (2 hours)
PHASE 9   CONSOLIDATION             16:00 – 17:00  (1 hour)
BUFFER                              17:00 – EXAM
```

---

### Phase 1: Deep Triage (1 hour)

Extends the 24-hour triage with past paper analysis.

**Tools:** Claude (past paper analysis) + ChatGPT (schedule generation)

```
PART A: Past paper pattern extraction (30 min)
Tool: Claude
Prompt: "Analyze these past papers: [PASTE OR DESCRIBE PAPERS].
         For each year, list every question.
         Then identify:
         1. Topics that appear in 3+ of 5 papers (High probability)
         2. Topics that appear in 1–2 papers (Moderate)
         3. Topics that never appear (Skip)
         4. Question types (define/explain/apply/calculate/derive)
         5. Marks distribution across topics
         Give me a priority ranked topic list based on
         5-year frequency."

PART B: 48-hour schedule generation (30 min)
Tool: ChatGPT
Input: Ranked topic list from Part A
Prompt: "Build me a 48-hour study schedule for [SUBJECT].
         Topics ranked by priority: [PASTE LIST].
         Time available: [YOUR HOURS].
         My target marks: [TARGET].
         Include: topic, time block, tool to use,
         specific task for each block.
         Flag where I should take breaks."
```

---

### Phases 2–9: Extended Coverage

**Phase 2 (Critical Topic Mastery — 8 hours):**
Same per-topic sequence as 24-hour workflow, but time per Critical topic extends to 75 minutes:

```
Per Critical Topic (75 min):
  0–15 min:  Conceptual foundation (ChatGPT)
  15–35 min: Deep understanding / Why (Claude)
  35–50 min: Application practice — work through examples
  50–65 min: Self-generated practice question + answer
  65–75 min: Active recall without notes
```

**Phase 3 (Moderate Topic Coverage — 4 hours):**
35 minutes per Moderate topic using the 24-hour per-topic sequence.

**Phase 4 (Active Recall — 4 hours):**
Identical to Phase 3 of 24-hour workflow, with an extended answer evaluation block.

**Phase 5 (Professor Calibration — 2 hours):**
This phase does not exist in the 24-hour workflow. 48 hours allows time to align answer style with professor expectations.

```
PART A: Professor vocabulary extraction (45 min)
Tool: NotebookLM (if notes uploaded) or Claude
Prompt: "Based on [PROFESSOR'S NOTES/PAST PAPERS],
         what vocabulary, frameworks, and answer structures
         does this professor use and reward? Give me:
         - 10 terms I should use (with their definitions)
         - The answer opening style this professor expects
         - The conclusion style that earns full marks
         - Any specific formatting requirements"

PART B: Answer style calibration (75 min)
Tool: Claude
Write 2 practice answers.
For each: paste to Claude with professor type information.
Get calibration feedback specifically on style, not just content.
Rewrite one section of each answer incorporating feedback.
```

## Workflow 3 — Topper Mode

**Trigger conditions:** Target marks 85%+. Preparation time > 5 days. Student has reasonable foundational understanding of the subject.

**Design philosophy:** Topper mode is not about covering more — it is about going deeper on what matters, closing every gap including edge cases, and achieving answer quality that goes beyond adequate into genuinely impressive. The differentiation at the top percentile is rarely content knowledge (most students at this level know the content). It is argument quality, precision of language, originality of examples, and ability to handle unexpected question angles.

---

### Master Timeline

```
DAY 1-2: FOUNDATION AUDIT
DAY 3-4: DEPTH AND EDGE CASES
DAY 5:   ANSWER EXCELLENCE
DAY 6:   EXAM SIMULATION
DAY 7:   CONSOLIDATION
```

---

### Day 1–2: Foundation Audit

**Purpose:** Identify every gap, including subtle ones. Most topper-level students don't have obvious gaps — they have precision gaps (slightly wrong definition, example that doesn't quite work) and depth gaps (understand the surface, not the mechanism).

```
TOOL: Claude (primary)
METHOD: Adversarial testing

Prompt: "I am preparing to score 90%+ on my [SUBJECT] exam.
         I believe I understand [TOPIC] well.
         Test this assumption aggressively.
         Ask me 10 progressively harder questions.
         Start at basic recall, escalate to application,
         then to evaluation and critique.
         After each answer, tell me: what's correct,
         what's imprecise, what a top student would add."
```

For every topic: identify and document precision gaps and depth gaps separately. Precision gaps (wrong details) are fixed first. Depth gaps (shallow understanding) require the conceptual workflow below.

---

### Day 3–4: Depth and Edge Cases

**Purpose:** Understand not just what the syllabus covers, but what the syllabus assumes you know. Engage with the edges, exceptions, and debates.

```
TOOL: Claude + Perplexity

DEPTH PROTOCOL per topic:
Claude: "I understand [CONCEPT] at the standard university level.
         What do I need to understand to perform at the top 5%?
         What are the nuances, debates, exceptions, or
         cross-topic connections that average students miss?"

Perplexity: "What are the most current debates or developments
             in [TOPIC]? Include academic sources."

Claude: "Here is an unusual exam question about [TOPIC]: [PASTE].
         Give me the ideal answer, then tell me why most students
         would give a weaker answer."
```

**Edge case topic sourcing:**
For every Critical topic, identify one edge case the professor might test. Sources: the last question on past papers (professors often put harder questions last), footnotes from lectures, topics the professor mentioned in class but didn't test yet (due for appearance).

---

### Day 5: Answer Excellence

**Purpose:** Lift answer quality from "correct and complete" to "impressively precise and analytically sophisticated."

```
THE EXCELLENCE PROTOCOL:

STEP 1: Write your best answer to a Critical topic question (untimed).
STEP 2: Have Claude evaluate it as if marking a competition.
        Prompt: "Mark this answer as if selecting the top 5%
                 of student responses. What would it need to
                 be genuinely outstanding — not just correct?
                 Where is it adequate but not exceptional?"
STEP 3: Write the improved version.
STEP 4: Have Claude compare both versions and identify exactly
        what changed and why the second version is stronger.
STEP 5: Extract the transferable techniques — what made it better
        can be applied to other answers.

Repeat for 3–4 Critical topics.
```

---

### Day 6: Exam Simulation (Full Paper Conditions)

```
Full past paper under strict exam conditions:
- Timed to the exact exam duration
- No tools, no notes
- Simulate everything including reading time

Then: Full evaluation with Claude on every answer.
Prompt: "I am aiming for 90%+ on [SUBJECT].
         Here is my full simulated exam: [PASTE ALL ANSWERS].
         Mark every answer strictly.
         For each: the exact marks I would receive, why,
         and what would have earned the additional marks I missed."

Calculate simulated score. If below 85%: identify the 2–3 answers
losing the most marks and repeat the excellence protocol on those
topics before Day 7.
```

---

### Day 7: Consolidation and Fine-Tuning

```
MORNING: Gap closure
Any topics still below confidence threshold: one targeted
Claude session using the adversarial testing prompt.

AFTERNOON: Memory consolidation
NotebookLM audio overview for passive revision.
Final review of key terms, frameworks, and answer openers.

EVENING: Stop studying.
Toppers who study until midnight before an exam
underperform their preparation. The last study session
should end at least 10 hours before the exam.
```

## Workflow 4 — Pass-Only Mode

**Trigger conditions:** Target marks 45–55%. Limited time (12–36 hours). Student's primary goal is to clear the subject, not excel.

**Design philosophy:** Pass-only mode requires the most ruthless prioritization of any workflow. The question is not "what is important?" but "what is the minimum I can know and still pass?" This is a survival mathematics problem, not an academic one. Identify the marks required to pass, identify the easiest path to those marks, protect that path completely, abandon everything else.

---

### The Pass Calculation

Before any study begins, run this calculation:

```
PASS THRESHOLD CALCULATION:
Total marks in exam: [X]
Marks needed to pass: [X × 0.45] (or institution's pass threshold)
Marks available from questions I feel confident attempting: [Y]

IF Y > pass threshold: study plan protects and polishes Y
IF Y < pass threshold: study plan adds minimum viable new content
  to bridge the gap — prioritized by effort-to-marks ratio only.
```

---

### Master Timeline (24-hour version)

```
PHASE 1   PASS CALCULATION           0:00 – 0:30    (30 min)
PHASE 2   SAFE TOPIC POLISHING       0:30 – 4:30    (4 hours)
PHASE 3   GAP BRIDGING               4:30 – 8:30    (4 hours)
PHASE 4   ANSWER FORMAT TRAINING     8:30 – 11:30   (3 hours)
PHASE 5   MARKS TRAP AWARENESS       11:30 – 12:30  (1 hour)
PHASE 6   EXAM SIMULATION            12:30 – 14:30  (2 hours)
REST                                  14:30 – EXAM
```

---

### Phase 1: Pass Calculation

```
TOOL: ChatGPT
Prompt: "I need to pass [SUBJECT] with [PASS MARKS]%.
         My exam has [MARKS] total marks in [TIME] hours.
         From my syllabus, I feel confident about:
         [LIST TOPICS YOU KNOW].
         I know nothing about: [LIST UNKNOWN TOPICS].

         Tell me:
         1. How many marks I can likely get from what I already know
         2. Whether that's enough to pass
         3. If not: which unknown topics give the most marks
            for the least preparation time
         4. Which topics I should completely ignore"
```

---

### Phase 2: Safe Topic Polishing

The student's already-known topics are their primary asset. Do not abandon them to learn new ones — polish them to ensure maximum marks recovery.

```
For each known topic (30 min each):
Tool: Claude
Prompt: "I know [TOPIC] at a basic level. I need to pass,
         not excel. Tell me: what are the exact components
         a pass-level answer to this question needs?
         What is the minimum answer that would score 50%
         of available marks? What are the easy marks
         I might be leaving on the table?"
```

Pass mode does not pursue 100% on any topic. It pursues 60% on many topics. The marks distribution matters more than topic mastery.

---

### Phase 3: Gap Bridging

New topic coverage strictly limited to topics identified in Phase 1's pass calculation as minimum-viable additions.

```
For each gap-bridging topic (45 min each):
Tool: ChatGPT
Prompt: "Teach me [TOPIC] in 30 minutes for a university exam.
         I need a pass-level understanding only — not mastery.
         Give me:
         1. The one-paragraph explanation I need to know
         2. One example I can use in an answer
         3. The most likely question and a 4-line answer outline
         4. The key term I must use to signal I know this topic"
```

---

### Phase 4: Answer Format Training

Pass-level students often fail not on content but on answer format. A student who knows 50% of the content but formats answers correctly can pass. A student who knows 70% of the content but writes disorganized answers may not.

```
TOOL: Claude
FOCUS: Every answer must have: opening statement, middle content,
       conclusion that answers the question.

Prompt: "I need to pass [SUBJECT]. My answers tend to be
         disorganized [OR: too short / too long / missing conclusions].
         For each of these question types [LIST], give me:
         - A structural template I can memorize
         - The minimum length that earns a pass mark
         - The one thing I must never forget to include"
```

---

### Phase 5: Marks Trap Awareness

Pass-mode students lose marks to avoidable errors — missing parts of multi-part questions, not attempting enough questions, skipping the conclusion.

```
TOOL: ChatGPT
Prompt: "What are the most common reasons students fail
         [SUBJECT] exams despite knowing the content?
         List 5 specific marks traps and how to avoid each.
         Focus on: exam format, answer structure, and
         typical professor deductions."
```

## Workflow 5 — Strict Professor Mode

**Trigger conditions:** Professor type identified as "Strict Checking" (≥ 6/10 on strict dimension). Any time window.

**Design philosophy:** Strict professor mode allocates significantly more time to answer format, terminology precision, and rubric alignment than any other workflow. For this professor type, how the answer is written is nearly as important as what the answer contains.

---

### Key Adjustments to Standard Workflow

```
ADJUSTMENT 1: Add rubric study as a mandatory early phase
Before studying content, spend 45–60 minutes analyzing
available marking schemes and model answers from past papers.
Build a personal rubric document: what earns marks, what loses marks.

ADJUSTMENT 2: Double the answer practice time
Standard workflows: 20% of time on answer writing.
Strict professor workflow: 40% of time on answer writing.
Every topic studied must result in at least one written practice answer.

ADJUSTMENT 3: Claude evaluation is mandatory after every practice answer
Every practice answer goes through Claude evaluation with the
strict marking prompt before moving to the next topic.

ADJUSTMENT 4: Terminology audit before exam simulation
Tool: NotebookLM (from professor's materials) or Claude
Prompt: "From my uploaded materials, extract the professor's
         preferred terminology for [ALL MAJOR TOPICS].
         For each concept: list the exact term to use and
         the incorrect or informal alternatives to avoid."

ADJUSTMENT 5: Structural template memorization
For every major question type this professor uses:
memorize an answer structure template.
These templates are used in the exam to ensure no structural
marks are lost regardless of content uncertainty.
```

---

### Strict Professor Phase 0 (Additional Phase): Rubric Analysis

```
TIME: 60–90 minutes (added before main workflow)
TOOL: Claude

PART A: Marking scheme analysis
If marking scheme available:
Prompt: "Here is the marking scheme for [SUBJECT]: [PASTE].
         Analyze it and tell me:
         1. How many marks are available for: definition /
            explanation / example / application / evaluation
            (identify the split)
         2. What specific components earn marks vs lose them
         3. What this professor never awards marks for
         4. The answer length that corresponds to full marks"

PART B: Model answer reverse engineering
If model answers available:
Prompt: "Analyze this model answer: [PASTE].
         What structural features does it have that earn marks?
         What vocabulary does it use that I should replicate?
         How does it open and close?
         What would I write differently without seeing this model,
         and would those differences cost marks?"
```

---

### The Strict Professor Evaluation Loop

Every practice answer undergoes this cycle before moving on:

```
WRITE → EVALUATE → IDENTIFY DEDUCTIONS → REWRITE ONE SECTION → CONFIRM

WRITE: Untimed first draft from memory
EVALUATE (Claude): Strict marks-based feedback
Prompt: "Mark this as a strict professor.
         Deduct marks for:
         - Missing definitional components
         - Informal or imprecise terminology
         - Missing conclusion or opinion
         - Structural disorder
         - Incomplete coverage of question components
         Show marks per deduction. Total out of [MARKS]."

IDENTIFY DEDUCTIONS: Note which deductions are patterns
REWRITE ONE SECTION: Fix the highest-marks deduction only
CONFIRM: Have Claude re-mark the rewritten section
```

## Workflow 6 — Numerical-Heavy Mode

**Trigger conditions:** Professor type identified as Numerical (≥ 7/10 on numerical dimension), or subject type is mathematics, physics, engineering, chemistry (calculation-dominant), statistics.

**Design philosophy:** Numerical preparation is fundamentally different from theory preparation. Understanding and performance are separate skills in numerical subjects. A student who understands a method cannot necessarily execute it under exam conditions without extensive practice. Volume, speed, and error pattern elimination are the primary variables — not conceptual depth.

---

### The Core Principle: Problem Types, Not Topics

Every numerical subject has a finite set of problem types. The exam selects from this set. Preparation should be organized around problem types, not around chapter or topic — because the exam will test problem types, not chapters.

```
PHASE 0: PROBLEM TYPE INVENTORY
Tool: ChatGPT
Prompt: "For [SUBJECT/TOPIC], identify every distinct problem type
         that appears in university exams.
         For each type:
         - Name of the problem type
         - Identifying features (how do I recognize it in an exam?)
         - Required formula or method
         - A worked example
         - The most common error students make"

OUTPUT: A Problem Type Master List — the organizing structure
        for all subsequent preparation.
```

---

### Master Timeline

```
PHASE 0   PROBLEM TYPE INVENTORY      0:00 – 1:00    (1 hour)
PHASE 1   FORMULA FOUNDATION          1:00 – 2:30    (90 min)
PHASE 2   TYPE-BY-TYPE MASTERY        2:30 – 10:30   (8 hours)
PHASE 3   MIXED PRACTICE              10:30 – 13:30  (3 hours)
PHASE 4   SPEED AND ACCURACY DRILLS   13:30 – 16:30  (3 hours)
PHASE 5   ERROR PATTERN ELIMINATION   16:30 – 18:00  (90 min)
PHASE 6   TIMED FULL PAPER SIMULATION 18:00 – 20:00  (2 hours)
PHASE 7   CONSOLIDATION               20:00 – 21:00  (1 hour)
```

### Phase 1: Formula Foundation (90 minutes)

Not memorization of formulae as text — understanding of when and why each formula applies.

```
TOOL: Claude
For each formula in the subject:
Prompt: "For the formula [FORMULA]:
         1. What does each variable represent?
         2. Under what exact conditions does this formula apply?
         3. What's a common mistake in identifying when to use it?
         4. What's the most common algebraic error when using it?
         5. Give me a memory hook for the formula itself."

OUTPUT: Personal formula reference sheet with application conditions.
```

---

### Phase 2: Type-by-Type Mastery (8 hours)

For each problem type from Phase 0, follow this sequence:

```
Per Problem Type (45–60 min per type):

0–10 min: Study one fully worked example with explanation
TOOL: ChatGPT
Prompt: "Show me a worked example of [PROBLEM TYPE] with
         step-by-step explanation. For each step, explain
         why that step is performed."

10–30 min: Solve 3 practice problems independently
No tools. Attempt from scratch. Check against solutions after.

30–40 min: Error analysis
TOOL: Claude (if errors made)
Prompt: "I attempted [PROBLEM TYPE] and made these errors:
         [DESCRIBE ERRORS]. Explain exactly what went wrong
         and give me the correct approach."

40–50 min: Repeat the problem type from scratch without guidance
If clean execution: mark as mastered. Move to next type.
If errors persist: flag for Phase 5 error elimination.

50–60 min: Speed attempt
Same problem type, faster. The exam has time pressure.
```

---

### Phase 3: Mixed Practice (3 hours)

**Critical phase — often skipped, most valuable.**

Mixed practice means: a set of problems where the student doesn't know in advance which type each problem is. This simulates exam conditions. Without mixed practice, students develop type-recognition blindness — they can solve problems when they know the type but fail to identify the type from the question.

```
TOOL: ChatGPT
Prompt: "Generate a mixed practice set of 15 problems for [SUBJECT].
         Include all problem types from [LIST].
         Do not organize by type — randomize them.
         Provide solutions after I've attempted the full set."

AFTER SOLVING: For every problem where type identification
was wrong or slow — add to the recognition drill list.
```

---

### Phase 4: Speed and Accuracy Drills (3 hours)

```
TARGET: Full-paper speed.
Calculate: [Total marks in exam] ÷ [Exam duration in minutes]
This gives the required marks-per-minute rate.

For a 3-hour exam worth 100 marks:
Required rate = 100/180 = 0.56 marks per minute
A 10-mark problem should take no more than 18 minutes.

SPEED DRILL PROTOCOL:
Generate 3 problems of each type.
Set timer to target time per problem.
Attempt under timer.
Log: time taken vs target time, accuracy.
Repeat problem types where time exceeded target.
```

---

### Phase 5: Error Pattern Elimination (90 minutes)

```
COMPILE all errors from Phases 2, 3, and 4.
Classify them:
  CONCEPTUAL ERRORS — wrong formula or wrong method applied
  ALGEBRAIC ERRORS  — correct method, arithmetic mistake
  FORMAT ERRORS     — missing units, wrong notation, skipped step

For each error category:
  Conceptual: Return to Phase 1 for the relevant formula.
  Algebraic: Targeted drilling of the specific algebra pattern.
  Format: Add to pre-exam checklist (lost marks, easily prevented).
```

---

### Phase 6: Full Paper Simulation

```
Simulate a full exam paper under exact conditions:
- Same duration as actual exam
- No tools, no notes, no calculator (unless permitted)
- Attempt every question in order

THEN:
TOOL: ChatGPT + Wolfram Alpha
Check all answers. Calculate simulated score.
Identify: which problem types cost the most marks.
These types get one final targeted practice session.
```

## Workflow 7 — Theory-Heavy Mode

**Trigger conditions:** Professor type identified as Theory-Heavy (≥ 7/10), or subject is predominantly essay/argument/framework based.

**Design philosophy:** Theory preparation is fundamentally an argument construction exercise. Students prepare best by building their ability to construct, defend, and evaluate arguments — not by memorizing content. The workflow is organized around intellectual positions (theories, frameworks, claims) rather than topics.

---

### The Theory Map (Phase 0, Mandatory)

```
TIME: 45–60 minutes
TOOL: Claude

Before any content study, map the intellectual landscape.

Prompt: "For [SUBJECT], map the major theoretical frameworks.
         For each framework:
         - Core claim / central argument
         - Key thinkers associated with it
         - Historical emergence context
         - Strongest critique
         - Which other frameworks it directly responds to or conflicts with

         Then: draw the relationships between frameworks.
         Which ones are in direct opposition?
         Which ones build on each other?
         Which ones are often conflated but actually distinct?"

OUTPUT: A Theory Map — the organizing structure for all study.
```

---

### Master Timeline

```
PHASE 0   THEORY MAP                  0:00 – 1:00    (1 hour)
PHASE 1   FRAMEWORK MASTERY           1:00 – 7:00    (6 hours)
PHASE 2   COMPARISON CONSTRUCTION     7:00 – 10:00   (3 hours)
PHASE 3   ARGUMENT PRACTICE           10:00 – 14:00  (4 hours)
PHASE 4   CRITICAL EVALUATION         14:00 – 16:00  (2 hours)
PHASE 5   ESSAY STRUCTURE TRAINING    16:00 – 18:00  (2 hours)
PHASE 6   TIMED ESSAY SIMULATION      18:00 – 20:00  (2 hours)
PHASE 7   CONSOLIDATION               20:00 – 21:00  (1 hour)
```

---

### Phase 1: Framework Mastery (6 hours)

For each major framework in the subject, follow this sequence:

```
Per Framework (60–90 min):

0–20 min: Framework internalization
TOOL: Claude
Prompt: "Explain [FRAMEWORK] from first principles.
         I want to understand it as its proponents would defend it —
         not as a textbook summary.
         Then explain the strongest version of the main critique.
         Be at the level of a serious university student,
         not a popular summary."

20–40 min: Thinker depth
TOOL: Claude or ChatGPT
Prompt: "For [KEY THINKER associated with FRAMEWORK]:
         What is their most important contribution?
         What did they say that was new or contentious?
         What exact quote or claim would most impress
         a theory-focused professor?"

40–55 min: Self-articulation test
Close all tools. Write: "In my own words, [FRAMEWORK] argues that..."
Continue for 3–5 minutes. Compare with notes.
If you can articulate it fluently: mark as understood.
If you can't: return to the Claude explanation and try again.

55–60 min: Critique rehearsal
Argue against the framework. The ability to critique a theory
demonstrates a level of understanding that merely describing it
does not. This is what earns marks in the top band.
```

---

### Phase 2: Comparison Construction (3 hours)

Theory-heavy professors love comparison questions. "Compare and contrast X and Y" is their most common format.

```
TOOL: ChatGPT (for table generation) + Claude (for synthesis)

For every pair of frameworks that are likely to be compared:

STEP 1: ChatGPT
"Generate a detailed comparison table between [FRAMEWORK A]
 and [FRAMEWORK B]. Cover: core assumptions, methodology,
 key thinkers, strengths, weaknesses, and their positions on
 [CORE TOPIC OF THE SUBJECT]."

STEP 2: Claude
"I'm preparing for a theory-heavy university exam.
 Beyond the surface differences between [A] and [B],
 what is the fundamental philosophical disagreement
 between them? What question do they answer differently?
 This is the depth a high-scoring answer demonstrates."

STEP 3: Student constructs personal comparison narrative
Write a 200-word comparison of A and B without notes.
This narrative becomes the mental structure for any comparison
essay question about these frameworks.
```

---

### Phase 3–6: Argument and Essay Training

```
PHASE 3: ARGUMENT PRACTICE
Practice constructing arguments, not just recalling content.
TOOL: Claude
For each major theoretical debate:
"I'll argue [POSITION]. You argue the strongest possible
 counterposition. We'll debate this topic.
 After 5 exchanges, tell me where my arguments were weakest."

PHASE 4: CRITICAL EVALUATION
Move beyond description and comparison to evaluation.
TOOL: Claude
"Which of these frameworks is most convincing for explaining
 [REAL-WORLD PHENOMENON] and why? Help me construct the
 best possible evaluative argument that would impress a
 theory-focused professor."

PHASE 5: ESSAY STRUCTURE TRAINING
TOOL: ChatGPT
For each likely essay question type:
Generate the ideal essay structure (opening, argument progression,
counterargument acknowledgment, conclusion).
Memorize the structure separately from the content.

PHASE 6: TIMED ESSAY SIMULATION
Two full timed essays under exam conditions.
Claude evaluation focused on: argument quality, thinker citations,
critical evaluation depth, and conclusion decisiveness.
```

# Part 2: Dynamic Workflow Adaptation

---

## How Workflows Should Adapt in Real Time

A static workflow is a plan. A dynamic workflow is a system. The Workflow Engine must respond to new information continuously — student performance, time elapsed, discovered gaps, and completed phases.

---

### Adaptation Trigger 1: Phase Running Over Time

```
IF student is more than 20% over allocated time for a phase:

ACTION: Trigger time recalibration prompt
"You've spent [X] minutes on Phase 2 and allocated [Y].
 We need to recalibrate.

 Option A — Compress: Skip LOW priority topics entirely.
             Spend maximum 15 minutes on remaining MODERATE topics.
             This protects Phases 3–7 at the cost of breadth.

 Option B — Accept reduced coverage: Mark remaining topics
             as 'skim only' and continue at current depth.
             Risk: some topics may appear on exam uncovered.

 Option C — Extend and compress later: Continue current depth,
             compress Phases 5–6 (memorization and simulation).
             Risk: less practice time.

 RECOMMENDED based on [PROFESSOR TYPE]: [OPTION] because [REASON]"
```

---

### Adaptation Trigger 2: Active Recall Failure Rate

```
IF student scores < 50% on Phase 3 active recall testing:

IMMEDIATE ACTION: Pause progression.

"Your recall rate suggests the Phase 2 coverage was not
 encoding at exam-ready depth. This is common and fixable.

 We're adding a 30-minute consolidation block before Gap Repair.
 For the 3 topics where recall was weakest, run this:
 [TARGETED CONSOLIDATION PROMPT]

 Your new timeline: [RECALCULATED SCHEDULE]"
```

---

### Adaptation Trigger 3: New Past Paper Discovered

```
IF student uploads a past paper not previously analyzed:

ACTION: Re-run triage analysis, flag changes.

"New pattern detected from [YEAR] paper:
 Topic [X] has now appeared in 4 of 5 papers analyzed.
 This upgrades it from MODERATE to CRITICAL.

 Recommended action: Add [X] to your current study block.
 Estimated additional time: [Y] minutes.
 Topics you can de-prioritize to compensate: [LIST]"
```

---

### Adaptation Trigger 4: Student Marks Themselves as "Confident"

```
IF student marks a topic as confident ahead of schedule:

ACTION: Early completion protocol.

"You've marked [TOPIC] as confident 20 minutes ahead of schedule.
 Those 20 minutes can be used:

 A — Test this confidence: Have Claude ask you the hardest
     possible question on this topic right now.
     If you answer it well, the time is yours.

 B — Bank the time: Add 20 minutes to Gap Repair phase.

 C — Start next topic early.

 RECOMMENDED: Option A. Confidence without verification
              is the most common cause of exam surprises."
```

---

### Adaptation Trigger 5: Exam Day Discovered to Be Earlier Than Input

```
IF student updates exam time and reduces available hours:

IMMEDIATE FULL RECALCULATION:
- Re-triage based on new time constraint
- Remove lowest-priority phases (typically Topper-Mode depth work)
- Compress all remaining phases proportionally
- Escalate to appropriate emergency workflow
- Present new timeline with explicit trade-offs stated
```

# Part 4: Making Workflows Feel Intelligent and Personalized

---

## The 7 Personalization Signals

A workflow feels personalized when the student reads it and thinks "this was made for me" rather than "this is a generic study plan." There are seven signals that create this perception:

**Signal 1: The student's professor is named, not described generically.**
"Study for your Corporate Law exam" feels generic.
"Study for Professor Sharma's Corporate Law exam — her papers
consistently test application over theory, average 3 scenario questions,
and she always deducts for answers without a legal opinion" feels personal.

**Signal 2: The skip list is specific and confident.**
The most powerful personalization signal is explicit permission to not study something. "You don't need to study Unit 8" — backed by the data showing it has never appeared — is more impactful than any study recommendation. It says the system actually knows this exam, not just this subject.

**Signal 3: The rationale connects to the student's stated goal.**
A workflow for a student targeting 90% reads differently from one targeting 55%. Every recommendation should include a sentence explaining why it serves the stated target: "We're spending 3 hours on answer quality because at 90%, the marks you're missing are in presentation, not content."

**Signal 4: Time estimates are specific and realistic.**
"Spend some time on Unit 4" generates no trust.
"Unit 4 requires 45 minutes at the coverage depth this exam demands — 20 min understanding, 15 min practice, 10 min recall test" generates trust.

**Signal 5: The workflow acknowledges trade-offs explicitly.**
Intelligent systems acknowledge that their recommendations cost something. "By skipping Unit 11, you're accepting a small risk that 5–8 marks could come from there. Based on the past paper frequency (0 of 5 years), this is a good trade." Students trust systems that admit to making trade-offs rather than pretending the plan is perfect.

**Signal 6: The emotional state is addressed.**
Exam preparation is not a rational process for most students. A workflow that opens with "You have 24 hours and that's enough for a passing grade if you follow this precisely" is more effective than one that opens with the first study task, because the emotional barrier (panic, overwhelm, despair) must be addressed before cognitive work can begin.

**Signal 7: The plan evolves.**
A plan that never changes feels like a template. A plan that updates when the student falls behind, discovers a gap, or uploads a new past paper feels like an intelligent system that's paying attention. Every adaptation event should be surfaced to the student with an explicit "we've updated your plan because [reason]."

---

## Language Patterns That Signal Intelligence

```
INSTEAD OF:                    USE:
"Study Unit 4"              →  "Start with Unit 4 — it's the most
                                likely topic based on past papers"

"Use ChatGPT for summaries" →  "Use ChatGPT for the concept load —
                                Claude will evaluate your answers after"

"Practice questions"        →  "Write your answer to this question
                                first: [SPECIFIC QUESTION]. Then we'll
                                have Claude mark it before moving on."

"Review your notes"         →  "Don't re-read your notes.
                                Have ChatGPT quiz you on them instead.
                                Re-reading builds recognition;
                                quizzing builds recall."

"You have 24 hours"         →  "You have 24 hours and 6 Critical topics.
                                That's 2.5 hours per topic — achievable
                                if we skip Units 8, 11, and 14."

"Good luck on your exam"    →  "You've covered 82% of what's likely to
                                appear. The remaining 18% isn't worth
                                the hours. Stop studying now."
```
